The gap this fills

Two monitoring layers exist. Neither sees the machine as a whole.

Equipment rarely fails because one sensor spikes. It fails because many sensors drift together in a way that only makes sense when you look at all of them at once — a small rise in bearing temperature, a slight drop in lube oil pressure, a minor lift in vibration. Individually unremarkable. Together, a story.

The existing stack checks each sensor against a fixed limit every 15 seconds, and runs XGBoost predictions on a handful of key tags. Both are useful and both are blind to coordinated multivariate drift. The autoencoder is the third layer: one model per equipment unit, every sensor in the input, trained only on healthy data.

Why an autoencoder, and why feedforward

An autoencoder compresses each reading through a narrow bottleneck and rebuilds it. Train it only on healthy data and it learns the relationships that hold when the machine is well. When those relationships shift, reconstruction gets harder and the error rises — that rising error is the anomaly signal. No labelled failures needed, which matters because real industrial faults are rare enough that supervised learning is not an option.

Feedforward rather than LSTM because within a 30‑minute window the ordering of readings does not change the assessment. It trains faster, needs less data, and the per‑sensor error falls out directly — which is what makes the diagnosis layer free. A temporal architecture is deliberately deferred to a later phase.

How it is put together

Nine packages, each one stage of the pipeline

The prototype was two Jupyter notebooks (still kept in old_autoencoder_code/ for reference). The current codebase is an installable package: 2,887 lines across 36 modules in src/autoencoder/, plus a 493‑line orchestrator and 1,615 lines of tests. Every stage is a pure function returning a dataclass, which is why the test suite can cover the pipeline without a live model.

The pipeline

Thirteen steps, in strict order

Order matters here, and the spec is explicit about it: scaling before cleaning so outliers cannot distort the normalisation; regime segmentation before cleaning so healthy low‑load windows are not judged against high‑load ones. What follows is what main.py actually executes, not what the spec asks for — the differences are called out.

Prepare and clean

steps 1–7
  1. 01Load and validate — CSV or Parquet, parse the timestamp column, sort by time, reject non‑numeric sensor columns. The March run read 1,382,400 rows × 161 columns.data/ingestion.py
  2. 02Drop low‑variance columns — variance ≤ 1e‑5 means a stuck sensor or a constant setpoint, which adds noise without signal. Removed tag_132 and tag_133, leaving 158.data/preprocessing.py
  3. 03Fit RobustScaler — (X − median) / IQR, fit on training data only and serialised for reuse. Refitting at inference would silently shift the input distribution.data/preprocessing.py
  4. 04Build 30‑minute windows — 120 rows at 15‑second intervals; the trailing partial window is dropped. 1,382,400 rows → 11,520 windows.data/preprocessing.py
  5. 05Segment operating regimes — KMeans (k=3) on load indicators, clusters re‑ordered so 0=Low and 2=High. Result: Low 4,269 · Medium 3,007 · High 4,244.data/regime.py
  6. 06Clean within each regime — Isolation Forest, then PCA reconstruction error, then Mahalanobis distance, each applied separately per regime and recombined. Every removal is logged.data/cleaning.py, cleaning/*
  7. 07Validate and report — 10 random sensor‑pair scatter plots before/after, a window‑mean histogram, a per‑step removal chart, and a self‑contained HTML audit report.cleaning/validation.py, reporting/

Train and calibrate

steps 8–11
  1. 08Split at window boundaries — 85 / 10 / 5, never inside a window, so adjacent timesteps cannot leak across the split. Gave 8,897 / 1,046 / 525.training/dataset.py
  2. 09Train with early stopping — one window (120 rows) per gradient update, Adam at 1e‑3, ReduceLROnPlateau, patience 15, best weights restored at the end.training/trainer.py
  3. 10Calibrate thresholds and baselines — re‑score the whole training set, take P90 and P99 of the window scores, and compute per‑sensor mean MSE on the test split as the ratio denominator.alerting/thresholds.py, training/trainer.py
  4. 11Save the bundle — weights, scaler, thresholds, metadata, training error distribution, sensor baselines, and training history. A model loaded without its matching scaler fails silently, so they version together.artefacts/serialisation.py

Score and alert

steps 12–13
  1. 12Score each window — check the equipment is running, forward‑fill gaps under 4 rows, reject any window with >5% nulls on a sensor, scale, forward pass in eval(), then per‑row and per‑sensor MSE. The window score is the 95th percentile of the 120 row errors.inference/pipeline.py
  2. 13Classify, persist, diagnose — Green / Yellow / Red against the saved thresholds, then the persistence rule before any alert leaves the system, then rank sensors by their share of the error.alerting/zones.py, alerting/persistence.py, inference/diagnosis.py

The last full run

11,520 windows in, 10,468 out

The run was on synthetic data — eight months of 160 tags at 15‑second intervals, generated with three cycling load regimes, correlated sensor groups, gradual drift on ten tags, five shutdown events and roughly 3% injected anomaly windows. It exists to exercise the pipeline end to end at realistic scale, not to prove detection accuracy.

Cleaning removed 9.1% overall, comfortably inside the 40% warning ceiling, and remarkably even across regimes — 9.0% / 8.9% / 9.4%. That evenness is what you want: it says the per‑regime approach is doing its job rather than deleting one operating mode wholesale.

Thresholds and zones

The error distribution is not the shape the design assumes

Thresholds are meant to come from a right‑skewed, unimodal distribution of training error — that is an explicit Phase 2 acceptance criterion, and it is why percentiles were chosen over mean ± k·σ. What the run produced is bimodal: a tall mass of easily‑reconstructed windows near 0.07, a second peak up at 1.40, and a thin middle.

Findings

Three things to fix before this can be trusted

All three surfaced while reproducing the run rather than from reading the code. Two are single‑line fixes with large consequences; the third is a design decision that needs a call.

F1

Validation and test contain only the highest‑load regime

blocking

run_cleaning_pipeline() loops over regimes and appends each regime’s survivors in turn, so cleaned_windows.npy comes out ordered by regime, not by time. split_windows() then takes contiguous slices. The result is that the 85/10/5 split cuts along regime boundaries instead of across them.

block boundaries in cleaned_windows.npy Low idx 0– 3885 load mean −0.578 range [−0.726, −0.304] Medium idx 3885– 6625 load mean +0.003 range [−0.276, +0.282] High idx 6625–10468 load mean +0.562 range [+0.300, +0.706] split boundaries train 0–8897 | val 8897–9943 | test 9943–10468 → val and test fall entirely inside the High block train load range [−0.726, +0.705] val load range [+0.302, +0.706] test load range [+0.311, +0.704]

Reconstruction error on this data is almost entirely a function of load — corr(score, load) = −0.960. The low‑load half of the data averages a score of 1.00; the high‑load half averages 0.13. So a split that isolates the High regime does not produce a harder or easier validation set, it produces a different distribution.

Score against operating load

What it explains

  • The persistent 1.43–1.61× val/train loss ratio — a distribution difference read as overfitting.
  • The bimodal training error distribution — two humps are two regimes, not healthy versus faulty.
  • Why the P90 threshold is effectively a low‑load threshold that no high‑load window will ever cross.
  • Why sensor_baselines.npy, computed on the test split, describes only high‑load behaviour — while it is the denominator for every error ratio at inference.
Fix
Carry regime labels through cleaning and stratify the split by regime, or shuffle window order before splitting. Stratifying is the better option: it keeps every regime represented in all three splits and makes regime‑specific thresholds possible later, which the spec already anticipates for >8,000 clean windows.
Also
construct_windows_with_metadata() exists and preserves per‑window timestamps, but the pipeline calls the plain construct_windows(). Wiring it in would let removed windows be checked against time — the spec’s “removals should not form continuous blocks” validation, which is currently unenforceable.
F2

The demo inference path scales already‑scaled data

blocking

step_clean() scales the dataframe before cutting windows, so every window in cleaned_windows.npy is already in scaled space — correctly, and the model trains on it that way. But step_infer() passes those same windows to infer_window(), which applies scaler.transform() again.

525 test windows, same model, same thresholds without re‑scaling (matches how the model was trained) score min 0.0681 mean 0.0891 max 0.1297 zones green 525 · yellow 0 · red 0 alert green — below persistence thresholds as main.py runs it (scaler applied a second time) score min 3.8370 mean 3.8724 max 3.9272 zones green 0 · yellow 0 · red 525 alert red — 525 consecutive red windows

Every score lands above P99, so the summary printed at the end of a pipeline run reports a red alert on healthy synthetic data. The same double transform is on the --infer-only path: the CSV branch scales the frame, builds windows, and then infer_window() scales once more.

Fix
Decide where scaling belongs and do it exactly once. Cleanest is to keep windows in raw units through cleaning and let infer_window() own the transform — that matches production, where a raw 120×N window arrives from the client. Failing that, add a pre_scaled flag to infer_window() and set it on both internal callers.
Guard
A regression test asserting that scoring a training window through the inference path reproduces its stored training error would have caught this, and would catch it again.
F3

Regime features silently fell back to three temperature tags

needs a decision

configs/default.yaml asks for regimes to be segmented on speed, power and discharge_pressure — the load indicators the spec names. The synthetic dataset has none of those columns. main.py filters the list to columns that exist, finds nothing left, and falls back to sensor_columns[:3] — which is tag_001, tag_002, tag_003, all temperature sensors.

On this dataset it happened to work, because the generator drives every sensor group from one shared load signal, so temperature is a clean proxy. On real plant data the first three tags alphabetically will be an arbitrary choice, and a fallback that logs nothing is the kind of thing that is only discovered downstream.

Fix
Log a warning when named regime features are missing, and prefer segment_regimes_unsupervised() — already implemented and tested, PCA on the full sensor set — over an alphabetical guess. The spec explicitly recommends exactly that fallback.

Scaling exploration

What the Ray benchmarks actually said

test.ipynb is a scratchpad for the distributed question: is Ray worth it here? The honest answer from the numbers so far is not yet, and it is worth writing down why.

The benchmark ran 908 SVDs of an 800×800 matrix serially in 45.4 s. The Ray version ran 9,008 of the same task — ten times the work — in 90.4 s on 14 CPUs. That is roughly a 5× effective speed‑up once the workload difference is accounted for, well short of the 14× the core count suggests, because NumPy’s SVD is already multi‑threaded and the workers end up competing for the same cores.

The later cells are more useful as understanding than as measurement: 500 tasks submitted against 14 CPUs, retrieved with ray.wait(), showing the raylet queueing excess work and recycling a fixed pool of worker PIDs in waves. That is the right mental model to have before wiring train_model_distributed() into anything.

For this workload the real parallelism opportunity is not one model trained across many cores — it is many units’ models trained at once. One model per equipment unit is the architecture, so unit‑level parallelism is embarrassingly parallel and needs none of the gradient coordination TorchTrainer provides.

Next

In this order

  1. 01Stratify the split by regime — carry regime labels out of cleaning and split within each. Then re‑read the val/train ratio and the error distribution shape; both Phase 2 gates should move together.
  2. 02Scale exactly once — and add the round‑trip regression test, so a training window scored through the inference path reproduces its stored training error.
  3. 03Re‑calibrate thresholds on the fixed split — and check whether the Yellow band is still 4.7% wide. If the distribution stays bimodal after the split fix, regime‑stratified thresholds are the answer; the spec already allows them above 8,000 clean windows.
  4. 04Wire the transient filter — needs a nominated speed tag and its operating range per equipment type. Closes a Phase 1 criterion with code that already exists.
  5. 05Persist inference results — the one change that unblocks the entire monitoring package: drift detection, the weekly health log and all three retrain triggers.
  6. 06Complete the artefact bundle — equipment ID, training date and a version tag in the metadata, so per‑unit models can be told apart.
  7. 07Finish the persistence rules — the mixed Red+Yellow 2‑hour window and the all‑clear notification, both specified and both currently unreachable.
  8. 08Then move to real data — the synthetic set has served its purpose. Detection quality cannot be judged until the model runs against a unit with at least one known historical fault event.
